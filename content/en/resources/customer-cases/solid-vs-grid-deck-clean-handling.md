A packaging warehouse handles both small cartons and intact paper sacks. Conveyor components beneath the load path are costly to clean, so the operating team wants to reduce the chance of packaging fragments, dust or an accidental leak reaching the machinery. The pallet must also support routine cleaning and remain stable throughout the automated route.

*This scenario combines common handling requirements. It does not represent a specific food, pharmaceutical or customer installation, and it makes no hygiene-classification or regulatory-compliance claim. Regulated applications require a separate review of materials, design and cleaning procedures.*

## Begin with the load-to-deck interface

Small-footprint cartons, flexible sacks and soft packages can settle into an open grid. If the package base cannot span the openings, local deformation may affect stack quality and gradually shift the center of gravity as the load travels.

A [solid three-runner pallet]({{< relref "/product/solid-3runner" >}}) provides continuous support for these load types and can reduce the opportunity for small loose material to fall directly into the equipment below. That continuous surface also adds material and tare weight, and its drainage and friction behavior need to be considered.

## Grid decks offer airflow, drainage and lower tare weight

An open deck supports ventilation, rapid drainage and visual inspection. For rigid cartons and containers with a sufficiently broad base, a [grid three-runner pallet]({{< relref "/product/grid-3runner" >}}) can provide a defined machine-travel direction without adding solid surface where it is not required.

Openings also allow dust, fragments and small spills to reach rollers, chains, sensors and the floor. The project should consider the consequence of that material entering the system—not only how quickly the pallet itself can be washed.

## “Solid is more hygienic” is too broad a conclusion

A flat surface is often easier to wipe and makes residue more visible. It does not automatically meet a particular hygiene standard. Edges, fork openings, underside ribs and optional reinforcement remain part of the cleaning task.

For food, pharmaceutical or other regulated operations, the project may also need to address material documentation, color control, cleaning-agent compatibility, foreign-material control and traceability. A product photograph or general web description cannot replace the operator's compliance review.

## The cleaning process can change the preferred structure

Manual wiping, low-pressure rinsing, automatic pallet washers and chemical cleaning create different requirements. Water temperature, pressure, cleaning agent, drying method and cleaning frequency should be recorded. Anti-slip inserts, identification marks and steel reinforcement must also be compatible with the process.

If a solid deck can retain water, its storage angle and drying method need attention. If a grid deck is used, the openings should be checked for package-edge trapping and any effect on optical sensing.

## Include equipment maintenance in the cost comparison

Purchase price is only one part of lifecycle cost. The comparison should include pallet-cleaning time, conveyor-cleaning frequency, downtime following package damage and the effect of pallet tare weight on handling and machine capacity.

A simple decision sheet can ask:

- Can the unit-load base safely span the grid openings?
- Is ventilation or rapid drainage required?
- What happens if loose material reaches the conveyor below?
- Can the cleaning process reach the deck and underside geometry?
- Is the added weight of a solid deck acceptable to the system?
- Are anti-slip features, numbering or traceability marks required?

## Decision outcome

For small cartons, bagged material and routes where protecting the equipment below is important, a solid deck is often the stronger candidate. Where drainage and airflow are priorities and the load base is stable, a grid deck may be the more efficient structure.

Deck style does not define rack capacity.
